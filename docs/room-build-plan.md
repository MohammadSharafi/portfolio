# From a room you look at to a room you live in

> Everything between where the room is now and a playable toy world with your work inside it. Ordered so each phase makes the next one easier.

## Read this before anything else

**Bruno Simon's room is not photorealistic, and that is the lesson.** It is stylised geometry with _extraordinary baked lighting_ and almost no runtime cost — unlit materials, few draw calls, everything resolved into texture ahead of time. What reads as "real" there is the light, not the materials.

Chasing photoreal PBR on the web is how portfolios end up at 40 MB and 20 fps. Your room already uses the right technique. The gap is execution, and the single biggest item is in Phase 0 below.

## Stop wasting the bake

_Phase 0 · do this first_

Measured, not guessed: **the Toronto skyline is consuming 94% of your lightmap**. It is 7,541 m² of building faces, Blender packs the atlas proportionally to surface area, and it wins by a landslide. Your desk gets 0.1% — about a 130 px patch stretched across the whole desktop. The atlas is also only 14% full.

That is why the bake looks soft. It is not the lighting.

> **Done — and it was worth more than predicted**
>
> The skyline is out of the lightmap, small props have a texel floor, and the layout is repacked to fill the square. Measured on the same objects before and after:
> **The desk went from about 16 to 176 texels per metre — eleven times the linear resolution.** Atlas coverage went from 14% to 66%. Every other surface in the room moved by the same factor, and small props like the mug and the CV now sit above the floor at 260 to 440 texels per metre rather than vanishing into a handful of pixels.
> Verified alongside it: the skyline has no island in the atlas at all, and every painted surface still owns a clean 0–1 UV, so no screen tiles its own content. **One step remains and it is yours — re-bake.**

```

[############### Toronto skyline -- 94% ###############][.....]
the whole room shares the last 6%  |  the desk gets 0.1%

How much of that atlas is used at all
[##14%##][.................................................]
the other 85% of 16.7 million pixels is blank
```

_**Measured off the model, not estimated.** The skyline is 7,541 m² of building faces and Blender packs the atlas by surface area, so it wins by a landslide. Excluding it should give every surface in the room about sixteen times the texel density._

- [x] **Exclude the skyline from the lightmap** `S` — It is self-lit and 26 m outside a window. It needs no baked lighting at all — give it a flat emissive material and drop it from the bake set.
- [x] **Re-pack the atlas so it fills the square** `S` — 85% of 16.7 million pixels are currently empty. Tighter island margins and a repack.
- [x] **Give small props a texel floor** `M` — A pencil should not get the same share as a wall just because it is small. Weight the pack so nothing lands under a usable resolution.
- [ ] **Re-bake and compare against the current render** `S` — Same camera, same settings. If it is not visibly sharper, something did not take.

## Make the light do the work

_Phase 1_

With the resolution problem gone, the lighting becomes the thing you can actually see. Right now the room is evenly blue-grey in every corner, which is the single clearest sign of a rendered space rather than a photographed one.

> **Done — the rig, and a third stale coordinate**
>
> The rig was two soft area lights from above at 520 W and 180 W, which lit every corner of the room to the same value. It is now a practical overhead, a warm pool from the desk lamp, cold light off the monitors and city glow through the window, with the ambient dropped from 1.6 to 0.6 so shadows are allowed to be shadows.
> **The desk lamp's light was 2.4 m away from the desk lamp.** When the lamp moved to the back-right corner, three things stayed behind at its old position: its power cable, its camera stop, and its light — so the room's warmest pool fell on bare desk. That is the third instance of one bug, so `check_lights` now fails the build on it, and has been confirmed to catch the original.

> **The ceiling is harder than it looks — read before starting it**
>
> Built, measured, reverted. A ceiling at 2.7 m needs the walls cut down to 2.7 m to be worth anything, and **the walls were quietly doing a second job: at 5.4 m they are what hides the Toronto skyline from the establishing camera.** Cut them and the sky plane's edge appears as a hard diagonal across the top-left of the shot, with buildings floating in the void beside the room.
> So this item now has a prerequisite nobody knew about: the outside world needs to be a backdrop that reads correctly from any angle before the room can have a realistic ceiling height. Worth doing — it is the largest remaining lighting gain — but it is a day's work rather than an hour's, and it is not the thing to start with.

- [ ] **Add a ceiling** `S` — Light currently escapes straight up, so nothing bounces back down. A ceiling is a huge soft reflector and will change the lighting character more than any other single edit. Hide it from the outside camera.
- [x] **Rebuild the light rig with a dominant source** `M` — One key that the room falls away from. Warm pool under the desk lamp, cold blue from the monitors, genuinely dark corners.
- [ ] **Make the skyline visible through the window** `S` — It reads as solid black right now, so the best surface in the room shows nothing.
- [ ] **Raise bake samples and check the noise floor** `S` — 128 was a first pass. Find the point where more samples stop changing the image.
- [ ] **Imperfection pass** `S` — Nothing in a real room is square to the world. Rotate books a degree or two, vary their heights, let a paper sit askew, scuff the floor near the door. Cheapest realism in the entire list.
- [ ] **Depth of field and colour grading** `M` — The postprocessing stack is already there. A shallow focus on the focused object and a graded palette do more for "real" than any material change.

## Fill the room

_Phase 2_

A room reads as real when it is full of things nobody would think to model. Not furniture — furniture is the easy part and yours is already there. It is the light switch, the socket behind the desk, the coat on the back of the door, the crumpled paper that missed the bin.

**One thing to understand before you commit.** A room you look at from outside only needs detail where the camera goes. A room you _walk around in_ needs detail everywhere, because the player can put their nose against anything. Your keyboard currently has 75 blank keycaps with no legends on them — invisible from the establishing shot, and the first thing a walking character would stand on. Deciding to make this playable raises the detail bar across the entire room, and that is a cost worth accepting deliberately rather than discovering later.

```
  back wall ------------------------------------------------
  |                       |mon||mon|         [window]      |
  |                 |laptop||kbd|    |CV|          [rack]  |
  |                  ======= desk =======                  |
  |[bookshelf]        (chair)                              |
  |     .....  walking route  .....                        |
  |             +---------- Persian rug ---------+         |
  |             +         [coffee table]         +  [plant]|
  |             +             [sofa]             +         |
  |             +--------------------------------+         |
  (open) ----------------------------------------- (open)   camera ^
```

_**The room to scale, from the coordinates in `build_room.py`.** Two walls, two open sides, 6.4 × 5.2 m. Note how much floor is empty: that space is the walking route, and it is why the room can take a great deal more clutter around its edges without crowding._

> **Done — the shell, and a fourth invisible bug**
>
> Light switch, two wall sockets, a radiator under the window, door hinges, a hook on the back of the door with a bag on it, and a window latch. The cables have run to a power strip on the floor for weeks; until now that strip was plugged into nothing.
> **Every fin of the radiator was inside the wall.** The room lies on the greater side of both walls, so standing proud of one means adding to the coordinate — subtracting looks equally reasonable in the source and buries the object in the plaster. It showed 7 mm of back panel and read as a blank white slab screwed to the wall, which is a thing that could plausibly exist, which is exactly why nothing looked wrong. `check_buried` now fails the build on anything swallowed by a wall or the door.

### The shell — what a room has and yours does not

- [x] **Electrical: light switch, wall sockets, a plug in one of them** `S` — A room with no switch and no sockets is a stage set. The cables already run to a power strip — give them somewhere to go.
- [ ] **Ceiling fittings: pendant or downlights, smoke detector, vent** `S` — Comes free with the ceiling in Phase 1, and gives the light somewhere believable to come from.
- [x] **Radiator or air conditioning unit** `S` — Every room is heated or cooled by something. Its absence is felt without being noticed.
- [x] **Door furniture: handle, hinges, doorstop, coat hook with something on it** `S` — A jacket or a bag on the back of the door is the single cheapest way to say a person lives here.
- [x] **Window detail: latch, sill dust, a curtain rail beside the blind** `S` — The window is the best surface in the room and currently the least detailed.

> **Done — the desk was built for a giant**
>
> The desk measured **3.5 × 1.3 m**. No desk is 1.3 m deep; 75 cm is the standard and 80 is generous. Everything standing on it had been sized to match, so the whole surface was internally consistent and wrong: a **106 cm keyboard**, an **86 cm laptop**, and monitor panels **109 cm** wide, which is a size no monitor has ever been made in.
> What made it invisible is that the chair, the mug and the mouse were all correct. A room where everything is 60% too big just reads as a room; a room where _half_ of it is reads as wrong without anyone being able to say why. It would have stopped being subtle the moment a 12 cm character could stand on a keycap — which is the whole of Phase 3.
> **Holding the top height fixed is the trick that made this affordable.** Every prop on the desk is positioned relative to `DESK_TOP`, so nothing had to move vertically — only in plan. The desk's dimensions are now five constants at the top of the builder (`DESK_W`, `DESK_D`, `DESK_BACK`, `DESK_FRONT`, `DESK_TOP`) and every other desk dimension is derived from them, so this class of drift cannot recur silently.
> **The checks caught what the eye did not.** Shrinking the desk by 60% left three props inside one another (`ix_cv` in the lamp, `ix_headphones` and `ix_notebook` in the laptop base) and left **eight of the nine desk camera stops aiming at empty air** — each one a click that would have flown the camera to nothing. Both are failures written after earlier bugs of exactly that kind shipped. All nine stops were recomputed from the new geometry.
> **And one they did not catch: the chair.** Everything standing _on_ the desk was rebuilt with it. The chair is not on the desk, so it stayed exactly where it was — and the desk's front edge had moved back 55 cm, leaving a chair marooned half a metre out in open floor, too far to reach the keyboard from. In a render that reads as a chair someone pushed back, because that is a thing chairs do. `check_paired` now measures it: a chair belongs to its desk, front edge to front edge, somewhere between 10 cm tucked under and 35 cm pushed back.
> **Where it could actually go was a measured answer, not a chosen one.** The establishing camera looks in high from the right, and its sightlines to the three screens cross the chair's depth at **x = −0.25** (laptop), **0.40** (left monitor) and **0.98** (right monitor). The chair is 77 cm wide and the gaps between those lines are 65 and 58 cm — so there is no position _between_ them it fits in. On the keyboard's centreline it hid **100%** of the laptop screen; 34 cm right of that, **47%** of the left monitor. Left of all three lines it is clear, and that is in front of the laptop — a place a chair genuinely lives. Ray-cast from the home camera against each panel's own triangles, the three screens now read **0%**, **0%** and **1%** occluded, and the 1% is a corner of the CV holder, not the chair.

### The desk — what a developer's desk actually holds

- [x] **Real-world dimensions on the desk and everything standing on it** `M` — A 27-inch monitor is 61 cm wide and a desk is 75 cm deep. Derive every measurement from a real product or a standard, and put the ones everything else depends on in named constants.
- [ ] **Desk mat under the keyboard and mouse** `S` — Almost universal, changes the whole read of the surface, and gives the props something to sit on other than bare wood.
- [ ] **Keycap legends** `M` — 75 blank keys. Painted with the same canvas-texture system already driving the screens and book spines, so the machinery exists.
- [ ] **Webcam, microphone on a boom arm, desk speakers** `M` — You work remote across time zones. The kit that implies should be on the desk.
- [ ] **The small hardware: USB hub, dongles, an external drive, a charging phone** `M` — With cables. Loose cable is the detail that most reliably separates a modelled desk from a photographed one.
- [ ] **Stationery that is not a pencil: stapler, tape, scissors, paper clips, a rubber band** `S` — Currently there is a pen cup with three pens. A real desk has a drift of small objects.
- [ ] **Personal objects: keys, glasses, a water bottle, a wallet** `S` — These are what make it your desk rather than a desk.
- [ ] **Sticky notes stuck to the monitor bezel** `S` — Real, universal, and they can carry readable text through the system you already have.

### Signs of life

- [ ] **A bin, with paper in it and one piece that missed** `S` — Nothing says occupied like a near miss on the floor.
- [ ] **A hoodie over the back of the chair** `M` — Soft geometry breaking a hard silhouette. Worth more than three more rigid props.
- [ ] **Shoes by the door, a backpack against the wall** `S` — Establishes that someone arrives and leaves.
- [ ] **A second mug that was never taken to the kitchen** `S` — One mug is set dressing. Two is a habit.
- [ ] **Shelf clutter: leaning books, flat stacks, binders, boxes, a speaker, a trophy** `M` — Your shelf is currently rows of upright books at even spacing, which no shelf on earth is.
- [ ] **Lounge life: a throw over the sofa arm, a remote, magazines, a controller** `M` — The lounge half is the emptiest part of the room.
- [ ] **Guitar accessories: picks, a capo, a strap, sheet music** `S` — An instrument with no accessories reads as decoration rather than something played.

### Micro-detail — the layer that survives close inspection

- [ ] **Fasteners and seams: screw heads, panel gaps, ventilation grilles** `M` — Manufactured objects are assembled from parts. Seamless ones read as toys — which is fine for the character and wrong for the monitor.
- [ ] **A power LED on everything that has power** `S` — Cheap, and it makes a room at night feel electrically alive.
- [ ] **Rubber feet, cable strain reliefs, cable clips on the desk edge** `S` — The small print of industrial design. Nobody sees them; everyone notices when they are missing.
- [ ] **Labels and printed marks on objects** `M` — Spines and screens already carry real text. Extend it to everything a manufacturer would print on.

### Surface history — texture work, not modelling

This is where the largest realism gain per hour lives, and it is entirely in the texture pipeline you already have. Clean surfaces are the strongest possible signal that something was generated rather than lived with.

- [ ] **Dust on every horizontal surface** `M` — Top of the monitors, the shelf, the skirting, the picture frames. Graded by how often the surface is touched.
- [ ] **Wear where hands and feet go** `M` — Worn armrests, a shiny patch on the chair seat, scuffed floorboards by the door and under the castors, a rubbed desk edge.
- [ ] **Marks: a coffee ring, fingerprints on the screens, smudges on the glass** `S` — A coffee ring on the desk is one decal and it does more than a week of modelling.
- [ ] **Dust in the lamp beam** `M` — Visible motes in a light shaft. Almost the definition of atmosphere, and cheap as a particle system.

### Keeping it affordable

All of the above pulls directly against Phase 5 below. These three techniques are what let you have both, and they need to be in place _before_ the object count climbs, not after.

- [ ] **Instance everything repeated** `M` — Books, keycaps, castors, screws, sockets. One mesh drawn many times costs almost nothing; many copies of the same mesh cost every time.
- [ ] **Detail levels by distance** `M` — The far wall never gets closer than three metres in tour mode — but it does once a character can walk to it. Budget accordingly.
- [ ] **Re-check the lightmap budget after every batch** `S` — Every new object claims atlas space. The Phase 0 measurement is not a one-off — make it a habit.

## Object by object

_Reference · work through with Phase 2_

What each object needs to survive being looked at closely. Tick the object when its list is done. Open each one for the specifics — these are the details that carry realism, not the shapes, which you already have.

- [ ] **Chair** `L` — 16 details
  - **Real stitching, not just piping.** Dashed stitch lines running beside every seam — double-needle topstitch. The piping you have is the cord; the stitching is what holds it.
  - **Perforated centre panels.** Breathable seat and back centres, solid bolsters. This material split is what makes a chair read as manufactured upholstery.
  - **Foam crown and compression.** The seat should sit proud in the middle and be flattened where weight goes. Uniform thickness reads as plastic.
  - **Hard shell on the back of the backrest.** Different material from the front, usually with a moulded logo recess.
  - **The recline mechanism at the pivot** — a visible bracket and a tension knob under the seat.
  - **A height lever** opposite the tilt lever you already have.
  - **Armrest detail.** A slide track, an adjustment button, and a textured or ribbed pad surface.
  - **Telescoping gas lift.** Two visible sections plus the shroud, not one cylinder.
  - **Castor construction.** Tyre in a softer material than the hub, a visible axle, a hub cap.
  - **Base spokes with a ridge** down each one and a slight taper — a flat spoke reads as cardboard.
  - **Elastic straps** behind the shell holding the headrest and lumbar cushion, with buckles.
  - **A certification tag** sewn into a seam. Every chair has one.
  - **Wear:** shiny worn patches on the armrest tops and the seat front edge.
  - **The gap** between the backrest and the seat — chairs are two parts, not one.
  - **Belt slots** in the backrest if you want the racing look to land.
  - **A floor mat** under the castors, or scuffed floorboards where it has rolled.

- [ ] **Laptop** `M` — 14 details
  - **Keycap legends.** Sixty blank keys is the most conspicuous omission on the desk.
  - **Backlight bleed** around each cap. A lit keyboard glows between the keys, not through them.
  - **A polished chamfer** on the lid and base edges. This single detail does more for "expensive aluminium object" than anything else.
  - **Ports, recessed.** USB-C either side, a headphone jack. Cut into the edge, with a visible wall thickness.
  - **Speaker grilles** flanking the keyboard, perforated rather than painted.
  - **Webcam dot and privacy LED** in the top bezel, plus microphone holes.
  - **Two hinge points with a gap**, not one continuous barrel, and a thermal vent between them.
  - **Glass over the display.** The screen should be inset behind a reflective layer with a black mask around it — not an emissive plane flush with the lid.
  - **Underside:** vents, screws, rubber strips rather than four dots, a regulatory text panel.
  - **The seam line** where lid meets base, consistent all the way round.
  - **Trackpad inset** as a cut line, not a raised rectangle.
  - **A charger plugged in**, cable running off the desk edge with a clip.
  - **Fingerprints** on the palm rest and trackpad, dust in the key wells.
  - **Slight lid flex** — a perfectly flat lid is a giveaway.

- [ ] **Notes and paper** `M` — 12 details
  - **Paper is never flat.** Corner curl, a slight bow across the sheet. Flat rectangles are the tell.
  - **Individual sheet edges** in a stack, not one solid slab with a paper texture.
  - **A bright rim** where the edge catches light — paper edges glow.
  - **Printed rules or grid** on the pages.
  - **Handwriting** — the canvas-texture system already drives your screens and spines.
  - **A dog-eared corner** on at least one sheet.
  - **Spiral binding** on the notebook: real wire loops through punched holes.
  - **An elastic closure band** and a ribbon bookmark.
  - **Sticky notes curl away at the bottom** — only the top strip is adhesive. A flat sticky note is wrong.
  - **A torn edge** where a page came out of the pad.
  - **Pen indentation** on the sheet below the one written on.
  - **A coffee ring** on a corner.

- [ ] **Books and the shelf** `M` — 13 details
  - **Rounded spines.** Hardbacks have a curved back, not a flat one.
  - **The cover overhangs the text block** on three sides. This lip is instantly recognisable and currently missing.
  - **Page edges are striated**, slightly yellowed, and never a flat face.
  - **Head and tail bands** at the top and bottom of the spine.
  - **Dust jackets** with fold-in flaps on some, bare cloth on others.
  - **Books lean** where the row is not full, and they lean against each other.
  - **Varying depth**, not just height — some should stick out past the shelf edge.
  - **A few laid flat** on top of the upright row.
  - **One pulled half out.**
  - **A bookmark or receipt** sticking out of the top of one.
  - **Creased paperback spines** with white crack lines, worn corners on the hardbacks.
  - **Irregular gaps** between books. Even spacing is the strongest sign of a generated shelf.
  - **Dust on the top edge** of the books that have not been opened.

- [ ] **Desk** `S` — 10 details
  - **An edge profile** — bullnose, chamfer, or applied edge banding. A square-cut edge reads as a rendered box.
  - **End grain on the edges.** Darker, more porous, and running the wrong way to the face. A wood texture that wraps continuously round a corner is wrong.
  - **A cable grommet** in the top, with cables through it.
  - **A cable tray** under the rear edge.
  - **Levelling feet** under each leg.
  - **Fixings underneath** — cam locks, screw heads, an unfinished underside.
  - **A gap where it meets the wall.** Furniture never sits flush.
  - **Grain running along the length** of the top, and differently on the edge banding.
  - **A scratch, a dent, a ring stain.** One of each, not a uniform wear layer.
  - **The front edge worn smoother** than the rest, where forearms rest.

- [ ] **Server rack** `M` — 12 details
  - **Discrete 1U and 2U units** with rack ears and screws, not one solid block.
  - **Rack rails with square holes** and cage nuts in the ones being used.
  - **Blanking plates** in the empty slots — a rack with visible gaps is an unfinished rack.
  - **Drive bays** with handles and latches on the front.
  - **Activity LEDs blinking at different rates.** Synchronised blinking is the giveaway; real ones are independent.
  - **Honeycomb or slot ventilation** across every front panel.
  - **A network switch** with a row of link lights.
  - **A patch panel** and coloured patch cables — this is the visual signature of a rack.
  - **A UPS at the bottom.** Heaviest thing lowest, always.
  - **Cable management** at the back: ties, an arm, and some genuine spaghetti.
  - **Label-printer labels** on each unit.
  - **Heat shimmer and dust** on top, and a fan hum in the audio mix.

- [ ] **Drawers** `M` — 9 details
  - **A consistent 3–4 mm reveal** around every face. This gap is the whole reason a drawer front reads as a drawer front.
  - **The face proud of the carcass**, not flush with it.
  - **Ball-bearing runners**, visible the moment one opens.
  - **A drawer box in a different material** from the face — usually cheaper and unfinished.
  - **Contents.** An open drawer must have something in it: cables, pens, receipts, a charger. An empty one is worse than a closed one.
  - **A recessed bottom panel** sitting in a groove.
  - **Handle fixings** — screw heads inside the drawer where the pull bolts through.
  - **One left ajar** by a centimetre. Nobody closes all three.
  - **The interior has to be modelled and baked open**, or it will be lit as though the drawer were still shut.

- [x] **Persian rug — Isfahan medallion, madder red** `L` — 17 details · built, and it was the biggest single win in the room
  - **This is the largest untextured surface you have** — 13.7 m² of flat blue-grey. Replacing it with a real Isfahan carpet changes the room more than any other single object, and it is the one piece that says where you are from.
  - **Structure, in the right order:** field, central medallion (_toranj_), corner spandrels (_lachak_), main border, and two or three guard borders either side of it. Getting this hierarchy right matters more than the motifs inside it.
  - **Four-fold symmetry is your way in.** A medallion carpet mirrors across both axes, so you generate one quarter and mirror it twice. The borders are a repeating motif along a path. This makes an intricate Persian design genuinely tractable to draw procedurally — which almost nothing else at this level of detail is.
  - **Isfahan palette:** madder red field, indigo blue medallion, ivory spandrels, with walnut brown and pomegranate yellow in the smaller motifs. Not primary red — madder is a deep brick with a brown undertone.
  - **Abrash.** Faint horizontal bands where the dye lot changed mid-weave. This is the hallmark of a hand-knotted rug and the clearest single signal that it is not machine-made or a printed texture.
  - **Pile direction.** The nap lies one way, so the rug is visibly lighter from one end and darker from the other. Anisotropic sheen along the weave. Miss this and it reads as wallpaper on the floor.
  - **Knot resolution.** A fine Isfahan runs 500+ knots per square inch. Your texture has to resolve individual knots at close range — this needs 2048 px minimum, and it is the one surface worth spending that on.
  - **Pile height and shadow.** Wool stands 5–8 mm proud. Give the surface a real normal map with knot-scale depth, or use fur shells at the edges.
  - **Motifs, if you want it to read as Isfahan specifically:** palmettes, arabesque vine scrolls, cloud bands, and boteh. Not geometric — that is a Turkmen or Caucasian look, not Isfahan.
  - **The fringe is the warp itself** continuing past the pile, knotted in groups. It is not a separate trim added to the ends.
  - **Fringe wear.** Uneven length, a few strands missing, tangled at one corner.
  - **Selvedge.** The long sides are bound with overcast wool, slightly thicker than the pile.
  - **Traffic wear.** Pile worn lower along the path between door and desk, and where the chair castors run.
  - **It does not lie flat.** Corners curl slightly, and there is a shallow wave across the middle.
  - **Furniture compresses it.** The sofa legs and the plant pot should sit in slight depressions, not on top of the surface.
  - **The room's palette has to move to meet it.** A madder red rug against a blue-grey and purple room will fight. Warm the wall accent or pull the rug toward the room's blues in the border.
  - **Built:** field, medallion, spandrels, three-part border, abrash, traffic wear and fringe, drawn procedurally at 2048 px and mapped once across the whole rug. The first field attempt thresholded a _product_ of two waves, which widens the line wherever either factor crosses zero — so the tracery came out as large blobs covering half the field rather than fine vine scrolls.
  - **Still to do:** pile direction, and the room's palette moving to meet the red.
  - **It cost 1.2 MB**, and would have cost 5.8 MB. A carpet is a photograph, not data, so it ships as JPEG — but the glTF exporter re-encodes any image without a recognisable filepath and hands back a PNG of identical pixels. It has to go through a real file.
  - **Give it a camera stop and a panel.** This is the one object in the room with a real story attached, and right now the room says nothing about where you grew up.

- [ ] **Keyboard** `M` — 12 details
  - **The keys are all the same size and they must not be.** Fifteen by five identical caps is the most visibly wrong thing on the desk. Spacebar, shift, enter, backspace, tab and caps are all different widths.
  - **Sculpted rows.** Keycaps are not a flat plane — each row has its own height and angle, dishing toward the fingers. This silhouette is instantly recognisable and currently absent.
  - **Cluster gaps.** A gap under the function row, and between the main block and the navigation cluster.
  - **Legends** on every cap, correctly positioned top-left.
  - **Case slope** of about six degrees, with flip-out feet at the back.
  - **Switch stems visible** in the gaps between caps when seen from a low angle — which is exactly the angle a toy character has.
  - **Backlight bleed** around each cap rather than through it.
  - **Shine on the most-used keys.** W, A, S, D, E and the spacebar polish first. This is a wear map, and it is the detail that says someone types here.
  - **Dust and crumbs** in the wells between keys.
  - **A caps-lock indicator LED.**
  - **A cable** exiting the back, or a receiver dongle if wireless.
  - **A wrist rest**, compressed where forearms sit.

- [ ] **Glasses** `S` — 9 details
  - **Lenses are curved, not flat panes**, and they sit in a groove in the frame.
  - **Anti-reflective coating** throws a green or violet sheen at grazing angles. This is what makes a lens read as a lens rather than as a hole.
  - **Refraction through the lens.** Whatever is behind should shift and scale slightly. Even a crude fake sells it.
  - **A bright caustic** cast on the desk beneath each lens.
  - **Hinges with visible screws**, and temple tips that curve down.
  - **Nose pads** on their own little arms if metal-framed.
  - **Folded, one temple over the other** — never both flat and parallel.
  - **Fingerprints and a smudge** on one lens.
  - **A case or a cloth** nearby. Glasses on a desk always have a companion object.

- [ ] **Monitors** `M` — 10 details
  - **Glass over the panel**, with the display inset behind it and a black mask between. Not an emissive plane flush with the bezel.
  - **Backlight bleed at the corners** in dark content. Utterly characteristic of an LCD and almost never modelled.
  - **A reflection of the room** in the dark parts of the screen. At night this is most of the screen.
  - **OSD controls** — a joystick nub or buttons under the right of the chin.
  - **A brand mark** on the chin.
  - **Ventilation slots** across the back, and a VESA plate with four screws.
  - **A cable entry cover** at the back where the cables gather.
  - **The bezel lips slightly over the panel** rather than sitting flush.
  - **Dust on the top edge** and fingerprints near the bottom corner.
  - **A sticky note** on one bezel.

- [ ] **Mug and what is in it** `S` — 9 details
  - **The handle joins with a fillet**, not an intersection. A torus pushed into a cylinder is the current state and it reads as two shapes touching.
  - **A foot ring** on the base — the unglazed ring a mug actually stands on, so it contacts the desk in a circle, not a disc.
  - **Rim thickness.** The wall has to be visibly thick and rounded over at the lip.
  - **Liquid.** A surface disc set below the rim, dark, reflective, with a meniscus curving up at the wall.
  - **A tide mark** inside, above the liquid line.
  - **Glaze pooling** thicker toward the base outside, thinner at the rim.
  - **A chip or craze lines** if it is a favourite mug — and it should be.
  - **Steam** that reacts to the mug being moved and stops when it is knocked over.
  - **A ring stain on the desk** beside the coaster, from the times it missed.

- [ ] **Desk lamp** `S` — 8 details
  - **The shade interior is a different material** — brighter, warmer, often white or brass. This is what makes the light pool feel like it comes from somewhere.
  - **A visible bulb** inside the shade.
  - **Thin shade edge.** Sheet metal, about a millimetre. A thick rim reads as plastic.
  - **Visible joints** — bolts or wing nuts at each articulation.
  - **Springs** along the arms if you want the architect-lamp look.
  - **The cable clipped along the arm**, then running down the post.
  - **An inline switch** on the cable, or a rocker on the base.
  - **Felt on the underside** of the base, and heat discolouration inside the shade.

- [ ] **Plants** `S` — 9 details
  - **They lean toward the window.** Phototropism is real, everyone has seen it, and nobody models it. Free realism.
  - **No two leaves alike** — vary length, width, curl and droop.
  - **A midrib and slight cupping** on each leaf. Flat leaves read as cut paper.
  - **Yellowing and brown tips** on the oldest leaves. A perfect plant is a fake plant.
  - **Mounded soil** with visible particles, not a flat disc, and pebbles or bark on top.
  - **A drainage hole and a saucer**, with a water mark in it.
  - **Roots showing** at the soil surface on a pot-bound plant.
  - **Dust on the upper leaf surfaces.**
  - **Translucency.** Leaves glow where light passes through them — the difference between a plant and green plastic.

- [ ] **Window — and the night behind it** `M` — 9 details
  - **At night a window is a mirror.** This is the biggest miss on the object. It currently shows black; it should show the room reflected in the glass with the city faint behind it.
  - **The view needs parallax.** A flat backdrop gives itself away the moment the camera moves. Layer the skyline at a few depths.
  - **A latch and a handle**, with a strike on the frame.
  - **A rebate and putty line** where the glass meets the frame.
  - **Sill dust**, a water mark, and a dead fly.
  - **Condensation** in the bottom corners.
  - **Slight glass imperfection** — a very gentle waviness in the reflection.
  - **Grime on the outside face**, clean on the inside.
  - **A curtain rail** above the blind, even if there are no curtains on it.

- [ ] **Guitar, sofa, headphones, whiteboard, door** `M` — 15 details
  - **Guitar — six strings of visibly different gauge**, thick to thin. Uniform strings are wrong at a glance.
  - **Guitar — frets, position markers, nut, saddle and bridge pins**, plus a rosette around the soundhole.
  - **Guitar — wear on the fretboard** and a scratch below the soundhole where a pick lands.
  - **Guitar — the top is arched**, not flat, and the strings sit above the fretboard with a visible gap.
  - **Sofa — cushions sag and compress** where people sit, and the seat cushions are not identical.
  - **Sofa — a gap between the cushions**, dark, with something lost in it.
  - **Sofa — piping and seams**, same treatment as the chair.
  - **Sofa — a throw over one arm**, with real folds. Soft geometry against hard is worth more than three rigid props.
  - **Headphones — ear pads compressed** and worn shiny where they meet the head.
  - **Headphones — a headband slider with detents**, set at an asymmetric position.
  - **Whiteboard — ghosting.** Faint traces of imperfectly erased writing. The most characteristic whiteboard detail and the easiest to add.
  - **Whiteboard — a marker tray** with markers and a grubby eraser, plus a magnet holding a printout.
  - **Whiteboard — it is glossy** and should reflect the room.
  - **Door — handle, escutcheon, hinges and a strike plate**, with wear around the handle.
  - **Door — a gap at the bottom**, architrave round the frame, and left open a few degrees.

- [ ] **Placement — the rules that apply to everything** `S` — 12 rules
  - **Nothing is parallel to a wall.** One to three degrees off, every object, no exceptions. This one rule does more than any amount of modelling.
  - **Things migrate to edges.** People push objects to the back of a desk and the sides of a room. The middle of any surface tends to be clearer than the perimeter.
  - **The desk has zones,** not an even scatter: a typing zone kept clear, a drinking zone to the dominant-hand side, a reading and parking zone at the far edge.
  - **Objects cluster in threes** and leave gaps. Evenly spread props are the signature of a generated room.
  - **Everything rests, leans or hangs.** If an object is doing none of those three, it will read as floating even when it is technically touching.
  - **Contact deforms.** Soft things compress under hard things — the rug under the sofa legs, the cushion under a book.
  - **Heavier lower.** The UPS at the bottom of the rack, the big books on the bottom shelf.
  - **The chair is never square to the desk.** Nor is it centred.
  - **Keep the traffic path clear** from door to desk to sofa. Real rooms have negative space where people walk, and it is a strong subconscious cue.
  - **Leave exactly one thing genuinely out of place.** A tidy room reads as staged; a chaotic one reads as art-directed chaos. One wrong thing reads as real.
  - **Put the detail where the camera goes.** Every camera stop and every place a character can stand deserves close-range detail. Nowhere else does.
  - **Check silhouettes, not surfaces.** Stand the camera at floor level and look along the desk. Overlapping silhouettes are what make a room feel deep.

- [ ] **Air** `M` — 7 details
  - **Air is why a room feels like a volume** rather than a collection of surfaces. It is the most underrated item on this entire page.
  - **Dust motes** drifting in the lamp pool and the window shaft, catching light only where a beam crosses them.
  - **Light shafts** from the window and under the lampshade. Billboard cones or a screen-space pass — true volumetrics are too expensive here.
  - **A very slight haze** so distant surfaces lose a little contrast and saturation. Two per cent is plenty; more and it reads as fog.
  - **Heat shimmer** above the server rack.
  - **Steam from the mug**, reacting to the mug being moved.
  - **Everything here must be additive and unlit** so it composites over a baked room without needing to be baked itself.

- [ ] **The dark end of the light range** `M` — 8 details
  - **Your atlas is an 8-bit PNG, so dark gradients will band.** This is a real and current defect. Bake to 16-bit EXR, tone map, then convert — or you get visible steps across every shadowed wall.
  - **Shadows must never crush to pure black.** There is always bounce. Black shadows are the single clearest sign of a game engine from 2005.
  - **Dark areas keep their colour** — blue from the window, warm from the lamp. Neutral grey shadows look dead.
  - **Aim for ten distinguishable steps** between black and mid-grey. If you cannot see ten, the toe of your tone curve is too steep.
  - **Dither the dark gradients** to break up 8-bit banding at display time.
  - **Inverse-square falloff** so far corners genuinely fall away. Linear falloff flattens a room.
  - **Contact shadows matter more than cast shadows.** The thin dark line where two surfaces meet is what makes objects sit on the floor rather than hover above it.
  - **Soft-edged light pools.** A hard circle under the lamp reads as a decal.

- [ ] **Textures** `L` — 12 details
  - **256 px will not survive a walking character.** It is right for a room seen from outside and too coarse for one seen from ten centimetres. Hero surfaces need 1024.
  - **A micro-normal detail layer** blended over the base at a much finer scale. This is the single technique that makes surfaces hold up at nose distance.
  - **Roughness variation matters more than colour variation.** If you improve one map, improve that one — the eye reads surface by how it catches light, not by its tint.
  - **Anisotropic roughness for brushed metal.** Real brushed aluminium stretches highlights along the grain. Isotropic roughness makes it look like plastic.
  - **Fabric needs sheen.** A fresnel-driven rim brightness — cloth glows at grazing angles. Without it, upholstery reads as painted foam.
  - **Correlate the maps.** Wood grain must appear in colour, roughness and normal together. Uncorrelated maps look like two materials fighting.
  - **Edge-wear masks driven by curvature.** Worn edges are lighter and smoother. This is the highest-value mask you can add.
  - **Grime masks driven by cavity.** Dirt collects in crevices and never on high points.
  - **Cavity or ambient occlusion maps** so crevices read dark independently of the lightmap.
  - **Per-instance variation.** Every book and keycap currently samples the same texture at the same offset. Randomise the offset per instance or the repetition reads as a pattern.
  - **Triplanar mapping** on complex meshes to stop the stretching that unwrapping leaves behind.
  - **Anisotropic filtering** on the floor. At grazing angles it blurs to mush without it, and that is exactly the angle a character on the floor sees it from.

## The character

_Game design · read before Phase 3_

> **The one rule everything below follows**
>
> **Every ability exists to reveal something about you, and every challenge is shaped like the thing it is about.** A game bolted next to a portfolio is two products, both unfinished. A game that _is_ how the portfolio is read is one product with a reason to exist. If a mechanic does not teach the visitor something true about your work or your history, cut it.

### Premise

**You play a small robot that Mohammad built.** Not a shrunken visitor, not a generic mascot — a desk assistant made by the engineer whose room this is.

This is worth insisting on, because the fiction does three jobs at once. It makes every tool diegetic: a scanner, a data jack and a grapple are things an embedded engineer would plausibly build, so unlocking them _is_ a statement about what you can do. It gives the character a voice that can comment on the room without breaking anything. And it means the room is the robot's whole world, which is exactly the emotional register a 12 cm character in a 6 m room needs.

### Scale and movement — the actual numbers

Get these wrong and nothing else lands. These are derived from your room's real dimensions, not picked by feel.

```
top of the window     235 cm   19.6x
top of the bookshelf  211 cm   17.6x  <- the career climb
top of the monitors   152 cm   12.7x
top of the CV         109 cm    9.1x
desk top               79 cm    6.6x  <- the cliff
chair seat             55 cm    4.5x  <- first real obstacle
sofa seat              42 cm    3.5x
jump reach             26 cm    2.2x  <- cannot reach the chair
the character          12 cm    1.0x
```

_**Why 12 cm is the right height.** The desk becomes 6.6× the character — the ratio a four-storey building has to a person. The bookshelf is 17.6×, which is why it can carry the whole career as a climb. Jump reach deliberately falls short of the chair seat._

| Property       | Value                | Why                                                                                                                                                            |
| -------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Height         | 12 cm                | The desk at 79 cm becomes 6.6× your height — the same ratio a four-storey building has to a person. A genuine cliff.                                           |
| Walk speed     | 32 cm/s              | About 2.7 body-lengths per second. True-to-scale walking would be 10 cm/s and take a minute to cross the room; every platformer cheats this and so should you. |
| Run speed      | 65 cm/s              | Crosses the 6.4 m room in about ten seconds. Fast enough to feel free, slow enough that the room still feels big.                                              |
| Jump height    | 14 cm                | Reaches a book lying flat and the rug edge. Deliberately _cannot_ reach the chair seat — that has to be earned.                                                |
| Auto step-up   | 1.8 cm               | Floorboard edges, cables and the rug border are walked over rather than jumped. Below this, terrain is texture; above it, terrain is level design.             |
| Capsule radius | 4 cm                 | Fits between keyboard keys and under the sofa. Set the collision gaps in the room to respect this.                                                             |
| Camera         | 50 cm back, 20 cm up | Near clip at 1 cm, or the character's own feet clip through it.                                                                                                |
| Seat height    | 4.3× height          | The chair is the first real obstacle and the natural place to teach climbing.                                                                                  |
| Falling        | No damage            | It is a toy. It bounces, tips over and gets up. This is a portfolio — frustration is the only real failure state.                                              |

### Abilities

Eight, each found somewhere that means something. Start with nothing but walk and jump — the room should feel too big before it starts feeling navigable.

- [ ] **Scanner — the eyes** `M` — the ability that replaces the info panel
  - **What it does.** Hold a key and nearby objects outline and label themselves. Release and the nearest one opens its panel. This is your existing click-to-focus system, made diegetic.
  - **Line of sight matters.** Labels must not show through the desk or a wall, or the room stops having geography.
  - **Range about 40 cm** — three body-lengths. Long enough to be useful, short enough that you have to go to things.
  - **What it says about you.** You build assistants that read things and explain them. That is literally your day job on the clinical AI.
  - **Found:** you start with it. It is the tutorial.

- [ ] **Data jack — the strongest content delivery in the game** `M` — plug into things
  - **What it does.** Plug into a real port — the laptop's USB-C, the server rack, the phone, the monitor — and the screen above you comes alive with a project.
  - **Why it is the best mechanic here.** The visitor physically climbs to a port and the thing they get is your actual work. Effort spent, content delivered, no menu.
  - **Each port shows something different.** Laptop → the terminal and your numbers. Rack → local-first inference. Monitor → the clinical dashboard. Phone → the mobile apps.
  - **What it says about you.** Systems integration is the through-line of your CV. The mechanic is the CV.
  - **Found:** the tutorial hands it to you at the laptop.

- [ ] **Grip pads — climb soft things** `L` — the first real unlock
  - **What it does.** Climb any surface with tooth — chair upholstery, the sofa, rug pile, cable insulation, the bookshelf's wood grain. Not glass, not brushed aluminium, not the laptop lid.
  - **The material rule is the level design.** Tag every material as climbable or not, and your room's existing material system already knows which is which. Fabric and wood yes, metal and glass no.
  - **This is the ability that opens the room vertically**, and it is why the chair is the first obstacle.
  - **Hardest thing in this document to build.** Needs surface detection, ledge grabs and transition animations. Budget accordingly.
  - **Found:** in the drawer, which you have to open first.

- [ ] **Grapple line — climb hard things** `M` — the answer to everything the grip pads refuse
  - **What it does.** Fires at marked anchor points — the monitor arm, shelf brackets, the lamp's elbow, the gas lift, curtain rail — and reels you up.
  - **Anchors are authored, not automatic.** Placing them by hand is how you control the routes, and it is far more reliable than surface detection.
  - **Pairs with the grip pads deliberately:** soft surfaces are climbed slowly, hard ones are crossed quickly. Two verbs, different rhythms.
  - **Found:** on top of the server rack, reachable only by the chair route.

- [ ] **Toy car** `M` — the speed contrast, and the nod to what inspired this
  - **What it does.** A small RC car parked under the desk. Drive it. Floor and rug only — it cannot climb, which is exactly the point.
  - **Why it earns its place.** After twenty minutes of careful climbing, being fast and reckless on the floor is a genuine change of pace. Games need contrast more than they need features.
  - **The rug becomes terrain.** Pile slows you, the fringe is a bump, the floorboard gaps rattle you.
  - **It is also a wink at Bruno Simon's driving portfolio**, which is the honest reference for this whole project.
  - **Found:** under the desk, in the dark. You need the torch first.

- [ ] **Torch** `S` — cheap to build, and it makes the room twice as big
  - **What it does.** A narrow cone of light. Opens up under the desk, inside the drawer, behind the bookshelf, inside the server rack, under the sofa.
  - **It doubles the explorable room for almost no modelling**, because dark places need far less detail than lit ones.
  - **It has to be a real dynamic light**, which is the one place the baked room needs a runtime light. Budget one.
  - **Found:** the first thing you get, right after the scanner.

- [ ] **Shoulder charge — the only way to move anything** `M` — you have no hands, and that is the design
  - **What it does.** A short run-up and a charge that transfers real impulse into a rigid body. That is the whole verb.
  - **No carrying, ever.** A 12 cm toy that can pick up a mug has no scale. Everything is pushed, tipped, rolled or dropped, and the room stays heavy.
  - **Mass matters.** The mouse shifts a centimetre a charge; the mug takes a dozen; the chair rolls freely because it is on castors; the monitor will not move at all. The player learns the room's weights by trying, which is the best kind of teaching.
  - **Your physics bodies already exist.** The mug, pencil and mouse are Rapier rigid bodies today. What is missing is the character being able to transfer force to them.
  - **This is what makes the room a toy.** Everything else in this document is a route or a reveal. This is the one ability that lets someone play with your room rather than in it.
  - **Found:** you start with it. It is the second thing the tutorial teaches, right after walking.

- [ ] **Paper glider — getting down** `S` — solves a problem you will hit immediately
  - **The problem:** once climbing works, every ascent ends with the player stranded on a desk with no way down but falling. That is a bad feeling and it will happen constantly.
  - **What it does.** Fold one of the desk papers into a glider. Slow, controlled descent from any height.
  - **It also makes height a reward** rather than a commitment — the view from the shelf is worth having if leaving is fun.
  - **Found:** on the desk, among the papers, once you first reach the top.

### Scenarios

Eleven, ordered roughly as a player would meet them. Each one is built around a real piece of your history, and in every case **the mechanic is the metaphor** — the shape of the challenge says the thing, so the panel does not have to.

- [ ] **1. Boot sequence — the tutorial** `S` — teaches walk, scan, plug · reveals the current role
  - You wake on the desk mat. Everything is dark except the monitors.
  - Walk to the laptop. Plug in. The terminal lights up and greets you by name.
  - **Teaches** the three verbs the whole game runs on, in under a minute.
  - **Reveals** March Health, the current role, and the fact that this room belongs to someone.
  - **Design note.** Never gate the tutorial. Anyone who wants to leave and read the written site must be able to at any second.

- [ ] **2. The climb — and the best idea in this document** `M` — the bookshelf is the career timeline
  - The bookshelf is 2.18 m — **eighteen times your height.** The tallest thing you will ever climb.
  - **Each shelf is a year, bottom to top.** Byte Group at the bottom, Robintel, Capgroup, March Health, and the award on the capping board at the very top.
  - Every book you climb past is a project. Scanning it as you pass tells you what it was.
  - **Why this is the strongest scenario here:** the player physically ascends your career, in order, and arrives at the award out of breath. No panel can do that. The climb _is_ the CV, and the effort is the point.
  - **Route:** brackets and leaning books with grip pads, the last shelf by grapple. Glide down from the top.

- [ ] **3. No fibre — the dark run** `M` — reveals where you started
  - The lamp is off and the screens are asleep. The room is almost black.
  - Cross from the door to the desk and switch the lamp on, navigating by the LED strip, the standby lights and the window.
  - **Reveals** the line already on your sticky notes: started in a village with no fibre and no bootcamp.
  - **The mechanic is the metaphor.** Working with almost nothing, and the first thing you do on arriving is turn the lights on for whoever comes next. Do not explain this. Let the player feel it and read the note afterwards.
  - **Design note.** Dark is not the same as unreadable. Keep enough light to navigate — the point is difficulty, not frustration.

- [ ] **4. The 3 a.m. page — timed** `M` — reveals delivery and reliability
  - The server rack starts blinking red and something in the room starts beeping.
  - Reach the rack, plug in, and work through three steps before the timer runs out.
  - **Reveals** the on-call side of the job — the 98% sprint delivery rate, the CI/CD ownership, zero security incidents. Claims that are dull as bullets and vivid as a countdown.
  - **Failure is a retry, never a reset.** Nobody loses progress on a portfolio.
  - **The only timed challenge in the game.** One is tension; three is a chore.

- [ ] **5. The pile — the rug is the level** `M` — where the realism work and the game design turn out to be the same thing
  - **Run the numbers first.** Wool pile stands 5–8 mm. Your character is 120 mm. That is 6% of its height — the equivalent of a person wading through 12 cm of undergrowth. The rug is not a flat surface with a picture on it; it is **terrain**.
  - **Crossing it is slow and loud in the legs.** Movement drops to about 40%, the character lifts its knees, and the footsteps go soft. The player feels the change before they understand it.
  - **Here is the good part.** The traffic wear already on your detail list — pile crushed flat along the path from door to desk, and where the chair castors run — becomes the **fast lane**. The worn track is the road. You are not designing a level on top of the rug; the rug's own history _is_ the level, and the texture that makes it look real is the same data that makes it play.
  - **The fringe is a thicket.** Knotted warp threads as tall as you are. Push through slowly, or find the gap where it has worn away.
  - **Things dropped on the rug sink in and vanish.** Anything knocked off the desk lands in the pile and has to be hunted for with the torch. This is where the dropping mechanic and the rug meet.
  - **The toy car hates it.** Wheels bog down, so the car owns the bare floorboards and the rug owns your feet. Two surfaces, two movement modes, one clean rule.
  - **Reveals** the rug itself — Isfahan, hand-knotted, where you are from. It is the only object in the room whose story is older than your career.
  - **Technically:** a walkable-surface tag on the rug material driving a speed multiplier, plus a mask sampled from the wear map for the fast lanes. Cheap. The expensive part is the rug texture, which you are building anyway.

- [ ] **6. Knock it off — the room is your toolbox** `L` — the mechanic that makes this a toy rather than a tour
  - **The rule:** you are 12 cm and you weigh nothing, so **you cannot carry anything — you can only push.** That single constraint generates every puzzle below, and it is honest to the scale.
  - **Pushing takes a run-up.** Shoulder-charge the mouse and it shifts a centimetre. Charge it five times and it goes over the edge. The effort is the point; instant object manipulation would make the room weightless.
  - **What you knock down becomes level design.** The mouse dropped onto the chair, then off the chair, is a staircase you built. A book pushed off the shelf lands flat and is a ramp. The pencil rolled across a gap is a bridge. **The player authors their own route out of your props.**
  - **The best one: the mouse is on a cable.** Push it off the desk and it does not fall — it dangles. Now there is a rope from the desktop to the floor, and you made it. Same trick with the headphone lead and the blind cord.
  - **The chair is a vehicle.** Castors, so charging it rolls it. Park it under the shelf and you have reached a place you otherwise could not. This is also the answer to "the chair should move by the character, not by a click".
  - **The drawer is a staircase** once opened, and opening it needs the chair pushed into position first. Two mechanics composing into a third is what makes a toy feel deep rather than wide.
  - **The laptop lid is a ramp when shut and a wall when open.** One object, two level layouts, and the player controls which.
  - **Consequences stick.** Everything knocked down stays down. The mug spills and the papers are ruined. Come back tomorrow and the room is how you left it — your store already persists state, so this is nearly free.
  - **Why this replaces a pure platforming challenge.** "Cross the room without touching the floor" tests whether the player is good at the controls. This tests whether they understand the room. The second is far more interesting, and it is the only mechanic here that could not exist in any other portfolio.
  - **Reward:** the toy car, parked under the desk where you need the torch to find it.

- [ ] **7. The spill** `M` — consequence, and a reason to care about the room
  - Push the mug too far and it goes over. Coffee spreads toward the papers.
  - Drag the papers clear before the stain reaches them. Succeed and you can read them; fail and they are ruined until the room resets.
  - **The papers are notes on a real project**, so the stakes are content the player wanted.
  - **This is the moment the room stops being scenery.** The first time something is genuinely lost by carelessness is the first time a player believes the world is real.
  - Pairs directly with the spill work in Phase 4.

- [ ] **8. The drawer** `M` — reveals the origin story
  - The drawer is shut and its handle is above your head. Solve it: ride the chair, or grapple the pull, or find the one left ajar.
  - Inside, in the dark, needing the torch: **the first phone you ever shipped an app on.**
  - **Reveals** the Byte Group and Robintel years — 50,000 downloads, the banking apps, where it began.
  - **A closed drawer is a promise.** Making the player work to open one and putting something genuinely personal inside is the cheapest emotional beat available to you.
  - **Also holds the grip pads.** The origin story is what unlocks climbing, which is a nice thing for it to mean.

- [ ] **9. The window — the endgame** `M` — reveals where you are going
  - The sill is the highest reachable point and needs every ability to get to.
  - From up there, Toronto. The one thing in the room that is not about the past.
  - **The hardest climb should lead to the thing you want most.** That is what makes it read as an aspiration rather than a line on a page.
  - **Reveals** the honest version already in your registry: the desk is in Türkiye, Toronto is next.
  - **Make this the last thing found**, and let the room say so when it is.

- [ ] **10 and 11. Six strings, and the whiteboard** `M` — the two that show how you think and who you are
  - **Six strings.** Climb the guitar and walk the strings. Each one sounds. A four-note phrase to match, and the room plays it back.
  - Reveals the part of you that is not work — and every portfolio needs exactly one moment that has nothing to prove.
  - **The whiteboard.** Reach it and the architecture diagram becomes walkable: stand on the boxes, follow the arrows between them.
  - Walking the request path from client to gateway to service is a better explanation of how you think about systems than the diagram is. **The player traverses your architecture.**
  - Both are small. Both are the kind of thing people screenshot.

### Systems around the play

- [ ] **Progress, secrets and photo mode** `M` — 7 details

## Seven forks, and what each one costs

_Decisions_

Everywhere the plan so far assumes one path, here is the path and its alternatives. Each has a recommendation, but the recommendation is mine and the decision is yours — several of these are taste rather than engineering. Tick a fork once you have chosen.

- [ ] **How the character is lit** `M` — 3 options
  - **Blob shadow and a matched tone.** One soft shadow under the feet, ambient light tinted to the room. Half a day. Looks slightly pasted-on if you stare. _Recommended to start._
  - **Light probes through the room.** Sample the baked lighting at a grid of points so the character picks up local colour as it moves — warm under the lamp, blue by the monitors. A week. This is what real games do.
  - **Abandon the bake, light everything in real time.** Perfect consistency, a much lower ceiling on quality, and it spends your entire frame budget on lighting. _I would argue against this one._

- [ ] **How climbing works** `L` — 3 options, and this is the most expensive fork in the document
  - **Anchor points only.** You place climb points by hand; the character moves between them. Cheapest by a wide margin, completely predictable, and it means every route is deliberate. A few days.
  - **Climbable volumes.** Invisible boxes around the chair, the shelf and the sofa that the character can climb freely inside. Middle cost, feels much freer, and the boxes have to be authored anyway.
  - **A real surface-detection system.** Any material tagged climbable, with ledge grabs and transitions. Weeks, and the one thing here most likely to eat a month. _Only worth it if exploring turns out to be what people actually do._

- [ ] **Where the camera sits during play** `M` — 3 options
  - **Third person, behind the shoulder.** Conventional, readable, and it puts the room's scale right in front of you. Needs collision so it does not end up inside the sofa. _Recommended._
  - **Fixed isometric, following.** Much simpler — no collision problem at all, since the camera never enters the room. Reads as a toy, which suits the premise, but loses the sense of being small.
  - **First person.** Most immersive, hardest to control at this scale, and it throws away the character you spent time choosing. _Not for this._

- [ ] **What the room is trying to look like** `M` — 3 options — this is taste, not engineering
  - **Believable, not photoreal.** Real proportions, real wear, stylised materials. What Bruno Simon does, what your room already is, and what the web can actually carry. _Recommended._
  - **Miniature diorama.** Lean into the toy scale — tilt-shift blur, slightly oversaturated, everything reading as a model on a table. Distinctive, memorable, and it makes the character's size the whole point.
  - **Photoreal.** Scanned materials, 4K textures, heavy post. Forty megabytes and twenty frames per second on a phone. _The trap this document opens by warning about._

- [ ] **How to fix the skyline's 94%** `S` — 3 options
  - **Drop it from the bake entirely.** Flat emissive material, no lightmap at all. It is self-lit and 26 m away, so it loses nothing. Simplest and best. _Recommended._
  - **Give it its own small atlas.** Keeps baked lighting on it if you ever want the buildings to catch a sunset, at the cost of a second texture.
  - **Weight the packer so it gets less.** Keeps everything in one atlas but keeps the complexity too, and it will drift again the moment the skyline grows.

- [ ] **How the content reaches the visitor** `M` — 3 options
  - **Panels, as now.** Click, a card opens with the text. Fast, accessible, already built, and it works identically in both modes. _Keep this whatever else you add._
  - **Diegetic only.** Everything is on a screen, a page or a spine inside the room; no cards at all. Beautiful, and it makes the content much harder to reach in a hurry.
  - **Both, with the panel as the fallback.** Screens and pages carry the content for people exploring, panels carry it for people reading. More work, and it is what the room is already halfway to doing.

- [ ] **How much of this ships at once** `S` — 3 options
  - **Realism now, game later.** Phases 0 to 2 make the room clearly better on their own and need nothing from anyone else. Ship, then start the game. _Recommended — the room improves while the game is still being built._
  - **Everything at once.** One big release. More coherent, and it means months with nothing live.
  - **Game first, realism after.** Proves the fun quickly. The risk is that a game in a soft-looking room is a game nobody looks at twice.

## Make the room respond

_Phase 4_

Everything you asked for. ## Make it survive contact with a phone
